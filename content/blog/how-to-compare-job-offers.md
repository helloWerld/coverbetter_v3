To compare job offers, put each offer in one table and compare the same items. These include base pay, bonus, equity, benefits, time off, schedule, location, the role, the manager, and growth. Turn every money item into a yearly dollar amount. Then score the items that are not money against the priorities you write down before you look at the numbers. The best offer is the one that wins on the items that matter most to you, not the one with the biggest salary.

Two offers at the same time feel like good news, and they are. But they also create pressure, because each employer wants an answer soon. A clear method helps you decide fast and with less regret.

## Step 1: Write Your Priorities First

Before you open the offer letters again, write down what you need from your next job. Do this first, so the biggest number does not decide for you.

Answer these questions on paper:

- What is the lowest yearly pay that covers your costs and savings goals?
- Do you need health insurance for a partner or children?
- How many days a week can you commute, and how far?
- What do you want to learn in the next two years?
- What kind of manager helps you do your best work?
- How much risk can you accept (for example, a new startup or a role with a large commission)?

Then rank your top five items. You will use this ranking in Step 4.

## Step 2: Get Every Offer in Writing

You cannot compare job offers well with verbal numbers. Ask each employer for a written offer that includes:

- Job title and start date
- Base salary and pay schedule
- Bonus target and how it is paid
- Equity grant, vesting schedule, and type (for example, stock options or restricted stock units)
- Signing bonus and any repayment rule
- Benefits summary (health, dental, vision, retirement plan, and match)
- Paid time off and holidays
- Work location and remote or hybrid policy

If a detail is missing, ask for it. A simple email works: "Thank you for the offer. Before I decide, could you send the benefits summary and the details of the retirement match?"

## Step 3: Compare the Money Side by Side

Turn every money item into a yearly number. This is the core of how to compare job offers fairly. Use a table like this one.

| Item | Offer A | Offer B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base salary | [amount] | [amount] |
| Target bonus (yearly) | [amount] | [amount] |
| Equity (yearly value of the vesting) | [amount] | [amount] |
| Signing bonus (spread over the years you expect to stay) | [amount] | [amount] |
| Retirement match (yearly) | [amount] | [amount] |
| Your health insurance cost (yearly) | [minus amount] | [minus amount] |
| Commute cost (yearly) | [minus amount] | [minus amount] |
| Total yearly value | [total] | [total] |

A few rules make the numbers more honest:

- Count a bonus at its target only if the company paid it in past years. Ask: "How much of the target bonus did people in this role receive last year?"
- Treat equity in a private company as uncertain. It can be worth a lot, or nothing. Many people count it at a discount or at zero for the main comparison.
- Compare health plans by your likely total cost: the premium, the deductible, and the out-of-pocket maximum. A cheap premium with a high deductible can cost more if you see a doctor often.
- Check the cost of living if the jobs are in different cities. Housing is often the biggest difference.

Taxes, equity, and retirement plans have complex rules. If a large part of an offer is equity, talk to a tax professional before you decide.

## Step 4: Score the Things That Are Not Money

Money is only part of the decision. Many people leave jobs because of the manager, the hours, or a lack of growth. Score each offer from 1 to 5 on the items you ranked in Step 1.

Multiply each score by its weight, then add the results. The total does not make the decision for you. But it shows you where each offer is strong and where it is weak.

## Questions to Ask Before You Decide

If you do not have enough facts to score an item, ask. Employers expect questions at the offer stage. These questions get useful answers:

- "What would a successful first six months look like in this role?"
- "How do people in this role usually grow? Can you give an example?"
- "How are raises decided, and how often?"
- "What are the normal working hours for this team, and how often do people work late?"
- "Can I talk with someone who would be my peer on the team?"
- "Has the team changed much in the past year?"

Also look at the company from the outside. Read recent news about the company. Check reviews from current and past employees, and read them for patterns, not single stories.

## Step 5: Negotiate Before You Choose

A second offer often gives you room to ask for more. If you prefer Offer A but Offer B pays more, ask Offer A to close the gap. Be honest and specific.

> Hi [Recruiter Name],
>
> Thank you again for the offer for the [Job Title] role. I am excited about [Company] and the work on [team or project]. I have another offer with a base salary of [amount]. [Company] is my first choice. If you can move the base salary to [amount], I am ready to accept this week.
>
> Best regards,
> [Your Name]

Only say you will accept if you mean it. For more on how to ask, read our guide on [how to negotiate salary](/blog/how-to-negotiate-salary).

## Step 6: Make the Decision and Close Both Doors Well

Look at your money table and your score table together. Then ask yourself two last questions:

- Which job would you regret turning down in a year?
- Which job fits the life you want right now, not only the career you want later?

When you choose, accept the offer in writing first. Then decline the other offer quickly and politely, so the company can move to its next candidate. Thank them by name and keep the message short. You may want to work with these people later.

## Common Mistakes When You Compare Job Offers

- You compare only the base salary and ignore benefits, bonus, and commute costs.
- You count private company equity as if it were cash.
- You decide before you have every detail in writing.
- You accept one offer verbally and then keep negotiating with the other company.
- You ignore the manager. Ask to meet the person you will report to if you have not.
- You let a short deadline force a decision. Ask for a few more days if you need them.

Offer rules and benefits differ by country and by state, so check the local rules that apply to your offer.
