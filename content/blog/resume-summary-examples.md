A resume summary is two to four lines at the top of your resume. It says who you are, what you are good at, and what result you bring. The best resume summary examples share one pattern: a job title, years of experience, two or three skills from the job post, and one result. Write it last, after the rest of your resume, so it reflects your strongest points.

## What a Resume Summary Does

A reader looks at the top of your resume first. The summary tells them in a few seconds which role you fit and why they should keep reading. It works like the headline of a news story.

A good summary does three things for the reader:

- It names the role, so the reader can file you in the right place.
- It gives the skills that match the job post.
- It shows proof with one result.

The summary does not replace your experience section. It points to it.

## The Formula Behind Good Resume Summary Examples

Use this fill-in formula. Change the parts in square brackets.

> [Job Title] with [number] years of experience in [industry or area]. Skilled in [skill from job post], [skill from job post], and [skill from job post]. [One result with a number, a scale, or a clear outcome].

Here is the formula filled in:

> Customer success manager with 5 years of experience in B2B software. Skilled in onboarding, renewals, and account planning. Kept a book of 60 accounts at high renewal rates and grew expansion revenue each year.

Keep it to two to four lines on the page. If it is longer, readers skip it.

## Resume Summary Examples by Role

Use these examples as models. Do not copy a result that is not true for you. Replace each detail with your own facts.

### Office and Operations

> Operations coordinator with 4 years of experience in logistics. Skilled in vendor management, scheduling, and inventory systems. Cut order processing time by redesigning the intake form and approval steps.

> Executive assistant with 7 years of experience supporting C-level leaders. Skilled in calendar management, travel planning, and board meeting preparation. Trusted to manage confidential projects and a team budget.

### Sales and Marketing

> Account executive with 6 years of experience in SaaS sales. Skilled in discovery calls, pipeline management, and Salesforce. Exceeded quota in [number] of [number] quarters.

> Content marketer with 3 years of experience in B2B technology. Skilled in SEO writing, email campaigns, and analytics. Grew organic blog traffic and built a newsletter from zero subscribers.

### Technology

> Software engineer with 4 years of experience building web applications. Skilled in TypeScript, React, and PostgreSQL. Led the move of a large codebase to a new framework with no customer downtime.

> Data analyst with 3 years of experience in retail. Skilled in SQL, Python, and Tableau. Built weekly sales dashboards that the leadership team uses for pricing decisions.

### Healthcare and Education

> Registered nurse with 8 years of experience in emergency and critical care. Skilled in triage, patient education, and electronic health records. Trained new nurses as a unit preceptor.

> High school math teacher with 5 years of experience. Skilled in lesson design, differentiated instruction, and classroom technology. Started an after-school tutoring program for students who needed more support.

### Trades and Service

> Licensed electrician with 10 years of experience in commercial projects. Skilled in blueprint reading, code compliance, and crew supervision. Completed projects on schedule with a clean safety record.

> Restaurant manager with 6 years of experience in high-volume dining. Skilled in staff scheduling, cost control, and guest service. Reduced food waste through better ordering and prep planning.

## Resume Summary Examples by Career Stage

Your level changes what you put first.

### Entry Level or Recent Graduate

You have less work history, so lead with your education, projects, and skills.

> Recent marketing graduate from [University] with internship experience in social media and event planning. Skilled in Canva, Google Analytics, and copywriting. Ran a campus campaign that filled every seat at a [type] event.

> Computer science graduate with project experience in Python and machine learning. Built a [project type] app used by [group]. Looking to grow as a junior developer on a product team.

### Career Changer

Name the new role first. Then connect your old experience to it.

> Former teacher moving into instructional design. 7 years of experience creating lessons for different learning levels. Skilled in Articulate Storyline, needs analysis, and adult learning methods. Completed a certificate in instructional design in [year].

> Project manager moving from construction into software. Skilled in scheduling, budgets, and stakeholder updates. Managed projects worth [amount] with many teams and vendors.

### Senior and Leadership

Lead with scope: team size, budget, and business results.

> Director of operations with 15 years of experience in manufacturing. Led teams of up to [number] across [number] plants. Skilled in lean methods, capital planning, and supplier strategy. Improved on-time delivery across the network.

> Engineering manager with 12 years of experience, including 5 in people leadership. Grew a team from [number] to [number] engineers and improved release frequency. Skilled in hiring, roadmaps, and cloud architecture.

## How to Write Your Summary in 5 Steps

1. Read the job post twice. Mark the skills that appear more than once.
2. List your top three results from your experience section. Pick the one that matches the job post best.
3. Fill in the formula with your title, years, skills, and result.
4. Cut every word that does not add a fact. Remove words like "hardworking" and "passionate".
5. Read the summary next to the job post. Make sure that the title and at least two skills match.

For the skills part, this guide on [resume keywords](/blog/resume-keywords) shows how to find the terms that a job post uses most.

## Summary vs. Objective: Which to Use

| | Resume summary | Resume objective |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | What you offer the employer | What you want from the job |
| Best for | People with related experience | People with no related experience |
| Example start | "Data analyst with 3 years..." | "Seeking an entry-level role in..." |
| Common in US resumes now | Yes | Less common |

If you have any related experience, even from internships, projects, or volunteer work, use a summary.

## Mistakes That Make a Summary Weak

- Soft words with no proof. "Results-driven team player" tells the reader nothing.
- No job title. The reader must guess what you do.
- Too long. Six lines is a paragraph, and most people skip it.
- The same summary for every job. Change the title and skills for each application.
- Facts that the rest of the resume does not support. Every claim in the summary must appear again below with detail.
- Weak verbs. Use strong [resume action verbs](/blog/resume-action-verbs) like "built", "led", and "reduced".

## Checklist Before You Save

- The summary starts with a job title that matches the role you want.
- It has a number for years of experience, or a clear level.
- It names two or three skills from the job post.
- It includes one result with a number, a scale, or a clear outcome.
- It is two to four lines long.
- It has no "I", no clichés, and no claim that you cannot prove.

Use these resume summary examples as a starting point, and then make each one specific to you. A summary with your real numbers and the employer's own words will do more than a polished line that could belong to anyone. When you change jobs or targets, update the summary first, then tailor the rest of your resume to match.
