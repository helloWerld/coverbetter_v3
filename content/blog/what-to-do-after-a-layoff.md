After being laid off, first get the facts in writing: your last day, final pay, severance terms, and benefits end date. In the first week, apply for unemployment benefits, choose your health insurance option, and make a basic budget. Save your work contacts and examples of your work if company policy allows it. Then rest briefly, update your resume and LinkedIn, tell your network, and start a structured job search.

A layoff is a business decision about a role or a budget. It is not a review of your work. Many skilled people lose their jobs in layoffs, and recruiters know this. The steps below help you protect your money, your health coverage, and your next opportunity, in a clear order.

## What to Do After Being Laid Off: The First 48 Hours

You may feel shocked or angry. That is normal. In the first two days, focus on facts, not decisions.

1. Ask for everything in writing: your last day, final paycheck date, severance offer, and when your benefits end.
2. Do not sign anything on the spot. Ask how many days you have to review the severance agreement.
3. Ask about unused paid time off, any bonus or commission you earned, and equity that has vested.
4. Ask if the company offers outplacement services, such as career coaching.
5. Ask if your manager or another leader will give you a reference.
6. Save personal contact details for colleagues you want to stay in touch with.
7. Save copies of your own documents, such as performance reviews and pay stubs, if company policy allows it. Do not copy confidential company data.

## Questions to Ask HR

| Topic | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Final pay | When will I receive my final paycheck, and what will it include? |
| Severance | How much severance is offered, how is it paid, and what is the deadline to sign? |
| Paid time off | Will unused vacation be paid out? |
| Health insurance | When does my coverage end? When will I receive COBRA information? |
| Retirement plan | What happens to my 401(k) and any company match? |
| Equity | What happens to vested and unvested stock or options? |
| Reference | How will the company confirm my employment to future employers? |
| Equipment | How and when do I return company equipment? |

Rules for final pay, paid time off, and severance differ by state and by employer. This guide is not legal advice. If the amounts are large or the agreement is complex, ask an employment lawyer to review it.

## Week One: Protect Your Money and Health Coverage

### Apply for unemployment benefits

Apply in the state where you worked, and apply soon. Many states do not pay for the time before you apply. Each state runs its own program with its own rules. The US Department of Labor has a page that links to [state unemployment insurance programs](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/unemployment-insurance).

### Choose your health insurance

Find out exactly when your employer coverage ends. Then compare your options:

- COBRA continuation coverage, if your employer offers it. It lets you keep the same plan, but you usually pay the full cost.
- A plan through the Health Insurance Marketplace at [HealthCare.gov](https://www.healthcare.gov/) or your state's marketplace. Losing job-based coverage usually gives you a special enrollment period, which has a time limit.
- Coverage through a spouse's or partner's employer plan.
- Medicaid, depending on your state and income.

Compare the total cost, not only the monthly premium. Check that your doctors and medicines are covered.

### Make a basic budget

List your income sources: severance, final pay, unemployment benefits, savings, and any partner income. Then list your essential costs. Calculate how many months you can cover. This number reduces anxiety, because you can plan with a real deadline instead of a vague fear.

Pause or reduce costs you do not need. Contact lenders early if you expect trouble with payments, because some have hardship options.

## Week Two: Prepare Your Job Search

### Rest for a few days

If you can, take a few days to recover before you start applying. A layoff is stressful. A rested mind writes better applications and gives better interviews. If the stress lasts, read our guide on [job search burnout](/blog/job-search-burnout).

### Update your resume

Add your most recent role and your results while they are fresh. Write down the projects, numbers, and feedback you are proud of. Do not mention the layoff on your resume. Your dates and results are enough. If the search takes a while, our guide to an [employment gap on a resume](/blog/employment-gap-on-resume) shows how to handle it.

### Update LinkedIn

Update your headline and your "About" section. Use the "Open to Work" setting if it fits your situation. You can make it visible to recruiters only, or to everyone.

### Tell your network

People cannot help you if they do not know you are looking. Send short, specific messages to former colleagues, managers, and friends in your field.

> Hi [First Name],
>
> I wanted to let you know that my role at [Company] was eliminated in a recent layoff. I am now looking for a new role as a [Target Job Title] in [industry or location]. In my last role, I [one specific result].
>
> If you hear of an opening, or if you know someone I should talk to, I would really appreciate an introduction. Thank you!
>
> [Your Name]

A public post on LinkedIn can also bring help from people you did not expect. Keep it positive: what you did, what you want next, and how people can help.

## How to Explain the Layoff

You will be asked why you left. Prepare a short, honest answer and practice it until it feels natural.

Example answers:

- "My position was eliminated when the company reduced the size of the operations team."
- "The company closed our office, and my role was part of that change."
- "After the merger, the company combined two teams, and my role was cut."

Then move to the future: "I am proud of what I did there, especially [result]. Now I want to [goal], which is why this role interests me."

Do not criticize your former employer. Do not over-explain. A layoff is a common event, and a calm answer shows maturity.

## Build Your Job Search Plan

Once the urgent tasks are done, give your search a structure:

- Define your target roles, industries, and locations.
- Set weekly goals for applications, networking conversations, and interview practice.
- Track every application in one place.
- Keep a regular daily schedule with a start and stop time.

Our [job search plan](/blog/job-search-plan) guide walks you through each step.

## A Checklist for the First Month

- I have my last day, final pay, severance terms, and benefits end date in writing.
- I reviewed the severance agreement before signing, and I know the deadline.
- I applied for unemployment benefits in my state.
- I chose a health insurance option before my coverage ended.
- I made a budget and know how many months I can cover.
- I saved personal contacts and allowed personal documents.
- I updated my resume and LinkedIn profile.
- I told my network what kind of role I want.
- I practiced my layoff explanation out loud.
- I set a weekly job search plan.

Rules for unemployment, severance, and health coverage differ outside the US. In other countries, check your national government's employment and benefits websites.
