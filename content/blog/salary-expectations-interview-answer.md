To answer "What are your salary expectations?", give a researched range for the role, not your current pay, and connect it to the job's scope. For example: "Based on my research for this role and my experience, I am looking for $85,000 to $95,000 in base salary. I am open to talking about the full package." First ask what range the company has budgeted. If they share it, you can respond to their number instead of guessing.

## Why Employers Ask This Question

The question is not a trap. Most employers ask it for practical reasons:

- They want to know if your expectations fit the budget for the role.
- They want to avoid a long process that ends with an offer that you will refuse.
- They want to see if you understand the market value of the role.

Your goal is to give an answer that keeps you in the process and does not set your pay lower than it should be.

## Step 1: Research the Market Range

Do this before your first call with a recruiter. You need a range for this role, at this level, in this location or remote market.

Use several sources and compare them:

- The pay range in the job post. Some US states and cities require employers to publish a range, so many posts now include one.
- Salary data on job sites and pay comparison sites.
- The [US Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/), which gives median pay by occupation.
- People in your network who do similar work. Ask about ranges, not their exact pay.
- Recruiters who contact you about similar roles.

Write down a low, middle, and high number for the role. Adjust for your experience, the company size, and the cost of living where the job is.

## Step 2: Choose Your Numbers

You need three numbers before the interview:

1. Your walk-away number: the lowest base salary you would accept.
2. Your target number: the salary that you want and can support with research.
3. Your stated range: the range that you will say out loud.

Make the bottom of your stated range equal to your target or a little above it. Employers often hear the low end of a range first. Do not make the range very wide. A range like "$70,000 to $110,000" looks like you have not done the research.

## How to Answer "What Are Your Salary Expectations?"

Use one of these approaches. Choose the one that fits the stage of the process and how much you know.

### Option 1: Ask for Their Range First

This works best in an early recruiter call.

> "Before I give a number, could you share the range budgeted for this role? That will help me see if we are aligned."

Many recruiters will share it. If the range fits, you can say: "That range works for me, depending on the full package."

### Option 2: Give a Researched Range

Use this when they ask again, or when you already know the market well.

> "I researched [Job Title] roles in [location or remote], and I have [number] years of experience in [key skill]. Based on that, I am looking for a base salary between [$X] and [$Y]. I am flexible depending on the benefits and the full package."

### Option 3: Delay Politely

Use this when you know very little about the role yet.

> "I would like to learn more about the responsibilities and the team before I name a number. I am sure we can find a figure that is fair if we agree that I am the right person for the role."

Do not delay more than once or twice. If you refuse many times, the recruiter may think you are difficult to work with.

### Option 4: Answer With the Full Package in Mind

Use this when the role has a large bonus, equity, or unusual benefits.

> "For base salary, I am targeting [$X] to [$Y]. I am also interested in how the bonus and equity work for this role, because the total package matters to me."

## Example Answers by Situation

| Situation | What to say |
|---|---|
| Early phone screen | Ask for their range first, then confirm if it fits. |
| They insist on a number | Give your researched range with a short reason. |
| You are changing careers | Base the range on the new role, and mention transferable skills. |
| You earn far below the market now | Talk about the market range for the role, not your current pay. |
| You earn more than the posted range | Say what matters most to you, and ask if there is flexibility. |

## What Not to Say

These answers often hurt your result:

- "Whatever you think is fair." This gives the employer all the control.
- "I need $X because my rent is high." Your costs are not the employer's reason to pay more. Talk about the value of the role.
- A number with no research. If you guess low, you may get an offer at that low number.
- Your current salary, when you do not have to share it. It can anchor the offer to your old pay.
- A number far above the market without a reason. It can end the process early.

## How to Answer on an Application Form

Many online forms have a required field for desired salary. You cannot ask a question there, so you must enter something.

1. Read the job post for a posted range. If there is one, enter a number inside it that matches your target.
2. If there is no posted range, enter the bottom of your researched range.
3. If the field accepts text, you may write "Negotiable" or "Open to discussion", but expect the recruiter to ask again.

Do not leave a required field blank or enter "0" or "1". Some systems filter on this field, so a fake number can remove you from the list.

## How the Question Fits in the Interview

The salary question often comes near the end of a first call, after questions like "[Tell me about yourself](/blog/tell-me-about-yourself)". Prepare it like other [common interview questions](/blog/common-interview-questions). Write it down, say it aloud, and practice it until it sounds calm.

Practice the number itself. Many people know their range but say it in a weak voice, or add "but I am flexible" too fast. Say the range, give one reason, and then stop talking.

## After You Give Your Range

Your answer is not a final agreement. The real negotiation happens when you get an offer. At that time, you know more about the role, and the employer has already decided that they want you.

If the recruiter says your range is above their budget, ask: "What is the range for this role?" Then decide if it still makes sense for you. You can also ask about other parts of the package. Examples are a signing bonus, extra paid time off, or a salary review after six months.

Pay rules, salary history rules, and pay range disclosure rules differ by state and city. Check your local rules if you are not sure what an employer may ask.
