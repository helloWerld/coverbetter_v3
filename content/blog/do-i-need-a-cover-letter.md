You need a cover letter when the job post or the application form requires one. You also need one to explain something your resume cannot, such as a career change, a gap, or a move. When a letter is optional, a short, tailored one usually helps more than it hurts. You can skip it when the employer says not to send one, or when the application has no way to attach it.

So the short answer to "do I need a cover letter" is: often yes, and rarely is it a mistake to send a good one. This guide shows when a cover letter is required, when it helps, when to skip it, and how to write one quickly.

## When You Need a Cover Letter

Send a cover letter in these cases. In each one, skipping it can hurt your chances.

- The job post says "please include a cover letter" or lists it as a required document.
- The application form has a required cover letter field.
- The post asks you to answer a question in your letter, such as "Tell us why you want to join our team."
- You apply by email to a person, such as a small business owner or a hiring manager. The email body works as your cover letter.
- The role involves a lot of writing, such as communications, marketing, grant writing, or law.

If the post gives instructions for the letter, follow them exactly. Some employers use those instructions to see whether applicants read carefully.

## When a Cover Letter Helps Most

Even when it is optional, a cover letter helps when your resume alone does not tell the full story.

| Your situation | What the letter can explain |
| --- | --- |
| Career change | Why you are moving and how your skills transfer |
| Employment gap | A short, honest reason and why you are ready now |
| Relocation | That you are moving to the job's city and when |
| Referral | Who referred you and why they thought you fit |
| Entry level or no experience | Skills from school, projects, and volunteer work |
| Overqualified on paper | Why you want this specific role |
| Small company or startup | Real interest in their product and mission |

For a career change, the guide on the [career change cover letter](/blog/career-change-cover-letter) shows how to make the link clear. For a first job, see the guide on a [cover letter with no experience](/blog/cover-letter-with-no-experience).

## When You Can Skip a Cover Letter

You can skip the letter when:

- The post says "no cover letter" or "do not include a cover letter".
- The form has no field for one and allows only one file, and the post does not mention it.
- You apply through a quick-apply button that sends only your profile.
- A recruiter contacted you first and asked only for your resume.

Even then, you can put two or three sentences of the same content in the form's "additional information" box, if there is one.

## Do I Need a Cover Letter if It Says Optional?

"Optional" means the employer will not reject you for leaving it out. It does not mean nobody reads it. Some hiring managers read every letter. Others read letters only for applicants they are unsure about. You cannot know in advance which kind of reader you will get.

Ask yourself two questions:

1. Is this a job I really want?
2. Do I have something to say that my resume does not already say?

If the answer to either is yes, send a short letter. If both answers are no, and you are applying to many similar roles, you can decide to skip it. Be aware that a letter can help when the reader is choosing between close candidates.

## Cover Letters and Applicant Tracking Systems

Many companies use an applicant tracking system to collect applications. A cover letter you upload often sits next to your resume in the recruiter's view. Some systems also make the letter text searchable, and some do not. The setup varies by employer.

So a letter gives you one more place to use the words of the job. It cannot fix a resume that does not match. The guide on [what an applicant tracking system is](/blog/what-is-an-applicant-tracking-system) explains how these systems usually work.

## How to Write a Short Cover Letter Fast

When time is short, write a short letter instead of no letter. Three paragraphs of about 200 words are enough for many roles.

1. Opening: the job title and one sentence about your strongest fit.
2. Middle: two short proof points that match the top needs in the job post.
3. Close: why this company, a request for an interview, and a thank-you.

Write one strong base letter. Then, for each job, change the opening, the proof points, and the company sentence. With a base letter ready, each new version takes much less time than a letter from a blank page. [CoverBetter](/) writes a first draft from your resume and the job post, which you then edit.

## Short Cover Letter Template

Copy this and replace each bracket.

> Dear [Hiring Manager Name],
>
> I am applying for the [Job Title] role at [Company]. With [number] years of experience in [main skill from the job post], I can help your team [main goal or task from the job post].
>
> At [Current or Past Company], I [specific achievement that matches a need in the post]. I also [second achievement or skill that matches another need], which is a key part of this role.
>
> I want to join [Company] because [specific reason: product, customers, mission, or team]. I would welcome the chance to talk about how I can help. Thank you for your time and consideration.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Your Full Name]
> [Phone Number] | [Email Address]

## When You Apply by Email

When you send your application in an email, the email body is your cover letter. Put the short letter in the body, not only as an attachment. Use a subject line with the job title and your name, such as "[Job Title] application: [Your Name]". Attach your resume, and attach the letter as a PDF only if the post asks for it.

## Common Questions About Format

- File type: use PDF unless the post asks for DOCX.
- File name: "[First-Last]-Cover-Letter-[Company].pdf".
- Greeting: use the hiring manager's name if you can find it. If not, "Dear [Team Name] Hiring Team" works.
- Length: one page or less. Most letters are 200 to 400 words.

## A Simple Rule

If the employer asks for a cover letter, send one. If the employer says not to, do not. In every other case, send a short, tailored letter for the jobs you want most, and decide case by case for the rest.
