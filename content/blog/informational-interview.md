Good informational interview questions ask about the other person's real work and advice, not about a job opening. Ask how they got into their role and what a normal week looks like. Then ask which skills matter most, what they wish they knew earlier, and who else you should talk to. Prepare 8 to 10 questions for a 20-minute conversation, and choose the 5 or 6 that fit best when the call starts.

An informational interview is a short conversation with someone who does the job, works at the company, or is in the field that interests you. You are not applying for anything. You are collecting facts and advice that you cannot find online. Often, you also make a new professional contact.

## How to Ask for an Informational Interview

Keep the request short and specific. Say who you are, why you chose this person, and what you want: a short call at a time that suits them. Make it easy to say yes.

> Hi [First Name],
>
> I am a [your role or status] interested in [field or role]. I read your [post, article, or profile] about [specific topic], and I would value your advice. Would you be open to a 20-minute call in the next two weeks? I can work around your schedule. I want to learn how you got into [field] and what skills matter most in your role.
>
> Thank you,
> [Your Name]

If you send this on LinkedIn, cut it to fit the connection note limit. Our guide on [how to message a recruiter](/blog/how-to-message-a-recruiter) has more tips for short messages that get replies.

## Informational Interview Questions by Topic

You do not need all 40. Pick the questions that fit your goal and the person.

### Questions about their path

1. How did you get into this field?
2. What did you do before this role?
3. What made you choose this company?
4. Was there a turning point in your career? What happened?
5. If you started again today, what would you do differently?
6. What do you wish you knew in your first year?

### Questions about the work

7. What does a normal week look like for you?
8. Which parts of your job take the most time?
9. What do you enjoy most about the work?
10. What is the hardest part of the job?
11. Which tools or software do you use every day?
12. Who do you work with most, inside and outside your team?
13. How does your team measure success?
14. What problems is your team trying to solve this year?

### Questions about skills and hiring

15. Which skills matter most for success in this role?
16. Which skills are hard to find in candidates?
17. What do strong new hires do in their first few months?
18. What experience would make a candidate look ready for this kind of role?
19. Are certifications or degrees important in this field, or does experience count more?
20. How do people in this field usually find their jobs?
21. What makes an application or resume strong for this type of role?
22. What mistakes do you see people make when they try to enter this field?

### Questions about the company and culture

23. How would you describe the culture on your team?
24. How do people here get feedback?
25. How do people usually grow or move up here?
26. How has the company changed since you joined?
27. What kind of person does well here? What kind of person has a hard time?
28. How do teams here work with remote or hybrid colleagues?

### Questions about the field

29. Which changes in this field do you expect over the next few years?
30. Which companies do interesting work in this field?
31. Which publications, newsletters, or groups do you follow?
32. Are there events or associations that you recommend?
33. Which roles are close to yours that I should also look at?

### Questions about your own plan

34. Based on my background, what gaps should I close first?
35. Is there a type of project I could do now to build relevant experience?
36. How would you describe my background to someone in your field?
37. What would you do in my position?
38. Do I sound realistic about my timeline?

### Closing questions

39. Who else do you recommend I talk to?
40. Is there anything I did not ask that I should know?

## A 20-Minute Plan for the Conversation

A plan helps you respect the other person's time and still get the most useful answers.

| Time | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Minutes 0 to 2 | Thank them. Give a 30-second summary of your background and your goal. |
| Minutes 2 to 8 | Ask about their path and their work (2 or 3 questions). |
| Minutes 8 to 15 | Ask about skills, hiring, and the company (2 or 3 questions). |
| Minutes 15 to 18 | Ask for advice about your plan (1 or 2 questions). |
| Minutes 18 to 20 | Ask who else to talk to. Thank them and end on time. |

Watch the clock. At minute 18, say, "I want to respect your time. I have one last question." If they want to keep talking, they will tell you.

## How to Prepare

- Read the person's LinkedIn profile, recent posts, and any articles they wrote.
- Read the company website and recent news.
- Write your 30-second summary: who you are, what you do now, and what you want to learn.
- Choose your 8 to 10 questions and put the most important ones first.
- Prepare a short answer if they ask, "How can I help you?" Have one specific request ready, such as a name of another person to talk to.
- Test your video link and audio if the call is online.

## Questions to Avoid

Some questions waste the time or make the other person uncomfortable:

- Questions you could answer with a quick search, such as "What does your company do?"
- "Can you get me a job?" or "Are you hiring?" at the start of the call.
- Questions about their salary. You can ask about pay ranges for the role in general if the conversation is open, but use care.
- Questions about private company information.
- Questions that criticize their employer or their choices.

## What to Do After the Interview

Send a thank-you message the same day or the next day. Mention one specific piece of advice and what you will do with it.

> Hi [First Name],
>
> Thank you for your time today. Your advice about [specific advice] was very helpful. This week, I will [action you will take]. I will also reach out to [Name they recommended], as you suggested.
>
> I will let you know how it goes.
>
> Best,
> [Your Name]

Then keep the contact alive. Send a short update in a month or two, when you act on their advice or when you get a result. People like to know that their help made a difference.

If you later see an opening at their company, you can ask if they would refer you. Our guide on [how to ask for a referral](/blog/how-to-ask-for-a-referral) shows how to do that without pressure.

## Common Informational Interview Mistakes

- You talk more than you listen. Aim for the other person to talk most of the time.
- You go over the time you asked for.
- You read your questions like a survey and do not follow up on interesting answers.
- You forget to ask who else you should talk to.
- You do not send a thank-you message.
- You disappear after one conversation and only return when you need something.

In some industries and countries, people call these "coffee chats" or "career conversations." The method is the same.
