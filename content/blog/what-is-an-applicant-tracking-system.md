An applicant tracking system (ATS) is software that employers use to collect, store, and sort job applications. When you apply online, the system reads your resume and pulls out details such as your name, job titles, and skills. Then it puts your application in a list for the recruiter. A person usually decides who moves forward, but the ATS controls what that person sees and how they search.

This guide explains what an applicant tracking system does, what it does not do, and how to prepare a resume that it can read correctly.

## What an Applicant Tracking System Does

Think of an ATS as a database with a front door. The front door is the application form on the company's careers page. The database is where your resume, your answers, and your status live.

Most systems do some or all of these tasks:

- Post the job to the company careers page and to job boards.
- Collect resumes, cover letters, and answers to application questions.
- Parse the resume, which means it copies the text into fields such as "Work experience" and "Education".
- Let recruiters search all applicants by keyword, job title, location, or other filters.
- Track each applicant through stages, such as "Applied", "Phone screen", "Interview", and "Offer".
- Send emails, such as the automatic confirmation you get after you apply.
- Keep records that the employer may need for reporting.

Common names include Workday, Greenhouse, Lever, iCIMS, SmartRecruiters, and Taleo. Small companies sometimes use the hiring tools inside their payroll or HR software. The features and the settings differ from one system to the next, and from one employer to the next.

## What an ATS Usually Does Not Do

You may have read that a robot rejects most resumes before a human sees them. Be careful with that idea. The behavior depends on how each employer sets up its system.

Here is what is commonly true:

- A recruiter often searches or filters the applicant list. If your resume does not contain the words they search for, you may not appear in the results.
- Some employers add knockout questions. For example, "Are you authorized to work in the US?" or "Do you have a current RN license?" A "no" can move the application out of the main list.
- Some systems give each applicant a match score. Recruiters can choose to use it or ignore it.
- Many recruiters read applications in the order they arrive, or they sort by date.

So the main risk is not a secret robot. The main risks are a resume that the system parses badly, and a resume that does not use the words of the job.

## How ATS Parsing Works

When you upload a resume, the system tries to find the parts. It looks for headings it knows, such as "Experience", "Education", and "Skills". It looks for dates next to job titles. It reads the text from top to bottom and left to right.

Parsing errors happen when the layout confuses that reading order. After a bad parse, the recruiter may see your job titles in the wrong place, or your dates in the skills field. Some application forms then ask you to fix the fields by hand. That takes your time and is a warning sign that the file is hard to read.

### Formatting that parses well

| Usually safe | Often causes problems |
| --- | --- |
| One column | Two or three columns |
| Standard headings ("Experience", "Education", "Skills") | Creative headings ("Where I've Been") |
| Text in the body of the document | Text in headers, footers, or text boxes |
| Common fonts, such as Arial, Calibri, or Georgia | Icons, charts, and skill bars |
| Dates in one format, such as "Mar 2023 to Present" | Dates in several formats |
| DOCX or a text-based PDF | Scanned PDFs and image files |

A two-column resume can parse correctly in some systems. But you do not know which system a company uses until you apply. A simple one-column layout removes that risk.

### Test your resume in 2 minutes

1. Open your PDF and select all the text. Copy it.
2. Paste it into a plain text editor.
3. Read the result from top to bottom.
4. Check that each job title, company, and date appears in the correct order.
5. Fix anything that is missing, mixed up, or shown as strange symbols.

If the text comes out clean, most systems can read it. If it comes out mixed up, change the layout before you apply.

## How to Write a Resume for an Applicant Tracking System

A resume that works well in an ATS is also a resume that works well for a person. The goal is clear text and the right words.

### Use the words from the job post

Recruiters search with the words they know. Those words are usually in the job post. If the post says "project management" and your resume says "ran projects", a search can miss you.

Read the post and mark the skills, tools, and titles that appear more than once. Then use the same terms in your resume where they are true for you. The guide on [resume keywords](/blog/resume-keywords) shows how to find and place them.

### Match your title where it is honest

If the job is "Customer Success Manager" and your title was "Client Relations Lead", you can add a short line under your title. For example: "Client Relations Lead (customer success, 40 accounts)". Do not change your real title. A background check can show the real one.

### Spell out acronyms once

Write "search engine optimization (SEO)" the first time. A recruiter may search for either form.

### Keep skills in plain text

Put a skills section near the top or bottom with simple comma-separated items. Do not use a chart or a star rating. A system cannot read a bar that shows "80% Excel".

### Tailor each application

One resume for every job rarely matches the search terms of each post. Change your summary, your skills, and the order of your bullets for each job. The steps are in [how to tailor your resume to a job description](/blog/tailor-resume-to-job-description).

## The Application Form Matters Too

Many people focus on the resume and rush the form. In many systems, the form answers go into fields that recruiters filter on.

- Answer every required question with care, especially work authorization, location, and years of experience.
- If a field asks for salary, read the guidance from your local rules and the post. Some US states require employers to show a pay range in the job post.
- Upload a cover letter if there is a field for it. Some recruiters read it, and it gives you one more place to use the words of the job.
- Check the confirmation email. Save it, and record the date you applied.

## A Quick ATS Checklist Before You Apply

- One-column layout with standard headings.
- No important text in headers, footers, tables, or text boxes.
- File type that the post asks for, or DOCX or a text-based PDF.
- File name with your name, such as "Jordan-Lee-Resume.pdf".
- Skills and tools from the job post, used where they are true.
- Acronyms spelled out once.
- Dates in one format.
- Copy-and-paste test passed.
- All form questions answered and checked.

## What to Do After You Apply

The ATS changes your status as the recruiter moves you through the process. Some systems send an email at each step. Many do not. A quiet application does not always mean a rejection, because some teams review in batches.

Keep your own record of each job, the date, the contact, and the next step. A simple sheet works well, and the guide on a [job application tracker](/blog/job-application-tracker) gives you a template. Your own record is the only one you control.
