Here is how to address a cover letter: use "Dear" and the hiring manager's full name, such as "Dear Ana Torres,". A title and last name, such as "Dear Ms. Torres,", is also correct. If you cannot find a name, address the team, such as "Dear Marketing Hiring Team,", or write "Dear Hiring Manager,". Avoid "To Whom It May Concern" and "Dear Sir or Madam", because they sound old and distant.

## How to Address a Cover Letter: The Options in Order

Use the first option that you can. Each one is a little less personal than the one before it.

| Order | What you know | Greeting |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The hiring manager's name | Dear Ana Torres, or Dear Ms. Torres, |
| 2 | The recruiter's name | Dear Sam Patel, |
| 3 | The name of the team or department | Dear Customer Success Hiring Team, |
| 4 | The job title of the person you report to | Dear Head of Engineering, |
| 5 | Nothing | Dear Hiring Manager, |

All five are correct and professional. A name is best because it shows that you did some research. If you spent ten minutes and found nothing, use a general greeting and spend your time on the letter itself.

## How to Find the Hiring Manager's Name

Try these places in order. Give the search about 15 minutes, then stop.

1. Read the whole job post. Some posts name the manager or say "You will report to [Name], our [Title]".
2. Check the email address in the post. An address such as "ana.torres@company.com" gives you a name.
3. Search LinkedIn for the company and the team. Use terms such as "[Company] head of marketing" or "[Company] engineering manager".
4. Look at the company's About or Team page.
5. Check the recruiter who posted the job on LinkedIn. If you cannot find the manager, the recruiter is a good second choice.
6. Ask someone you know at the company.
7. If the post allows it, call the front desk and ask: "Could you tell me who manages the [Team Name] team? I would like to address my application correctly."

If two people could be the manager, pick the one whose title is closest to the role, or use the team greeting instead. A wrong name is worse than no name.

## Using Titles: Mr., Ms., Mx., and Dr.

In the US, a full name with no title is now common and safe: "Dear Jordan Lee,". It avoids guessing gender. Use a title when you are sure it is correct.

| Title | When to use it |
|---|---|
| Ms. | For a woman, when you do not know her marital status. Use Ms., not Miss or Mrs., unless she uses one of those. |
| Mr. | For a man. |
| Mx. | For a person who uses this title. Check their profile or email signature. |
| Dr. | For a person with a doctorate who uses the title at work, for example in medicine, research, or universities. |
| Professor | For a faculty member, when you apply for an academic role. |

If a person lists pronouns or a title on LinkedIn or in their email signature, follow what they use.

## Colon or Comma After the Greeting

Both are correct. A colon is the most formal choice and is common in traditional business letters. A comma is more common in email and at most companies today. Use the same punctuation in every letter you send to one employer.

- Formal: "Dear Ms. Torres:"
- Standard: "Dear Ms. Torres,"
- Informal email: "Hi Ana,"

Use "Hi" only when the company is informal, or when the person has already written to you in an informal way.

## Greetings to Avoid

| Greeting | Why to avoid it | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| To Whom It May Concern | Sounds distant and old | Dear Hiring Manager, |
| Dear Sir or Madam | Assumes a gender and sounds old in the US | Dear Hiring Manager, |
| Dear Sirs | Assumes all readers are men | Dear Hiring Team, |
| Hey there, | Too casual for most applications | Hi [First Name], |
| Dear Ana, (first name only, formal letter) | Can sound too familiar to a stranger | Dear Ana Torres, |
| Dear Mrs. Torres, (guessing) | You may guess wrong | Dear Ana Torres, |
| No greeting at all | Looks unfinished | Dear Hiring Manager, |

Customs differ outside the US. In some countries, "Dear Sir or Madam" is still standard, so check local practice if you apply abroad.

## Addressing the Employer Block

When you send a cover letter as a file, put the employer's details above the greeting. Our [cover letter format](/blog/cover-letter-format) guide shows the full layout.

> [Hiring Manager Name]
> [Hiring Manager Title]
> [Company]
> [City, State]
>
> Dear [Hiring Manager Name],

If you do not know the name, start the block with the company name:

> [Company]
> [City, State]
>
> Dear Hiring Manager,

When you paste the letter into an online form or an email, skip the block and start with the greeting.

## Special Cases

### Two hiring managers

Name both: "Dear Ms. Torres and Mr. Patel,". Put them in the order the job post uses, or alphabetically.

### A whole panel or committee

Use the group name: "Dear Search Committee," or "Dear Members of the Hiring Committee,". This is common for academic and public sector roles.

### A referral

If someone referred you, address the letter to the hiring manager, then name your contact in the first sentence:

> Dear [Hiring Manager Name],
>
> [Referrer Name], who works on your [Team Name] team, suggested that I apply for the [Job Title] role.

### A gender-neutral or unfamiliar name

Use the full name with no title: "Dear Taylor Morgan,". This is correct for every reader.

### A recruitment agency

Address the letter to the agency recruiter by name. If you know the client company, mention the role and the client in the first line.

## Check Before You Send

- The name is spelled exactly as it appears on LinkedIn or the company site.
- The title (Ms., Mr., Mx., Dr.) is correct, or you used the full name with no title.
- The greeting matches the company's tone.
- The punctuation after the greeting is a comma or a colon, and it is the same in all your letters to this employer.
- The name in the employer block matches the name in the greeting.
- You did not copy the greeting from a letter to another company.

Learning how to address a cover letter takes a few minutes, and the greeting is the first line the reader sees. After the greeting, your opening line has to name the job and give a reason to read on.
