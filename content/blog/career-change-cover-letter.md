A career change cover letter explains why you are moving into a new field. It also shows how your past experience prepares you for the new role. Open with the job and a clear reason for the change. Then connect two or three transferable skills to the needs in the job post, with proof. Close by asking for an interview.

Your resume shows where you have been. Your cover letter shows why that history fits where you want to go. For a career changer, that makes the letter more useful than usual, because the hiring manager may not see the link on their own.

## What a Career Change Cover Letter Must Do

A hiring manager who reads your application will likely ask three questions:

1. Why does this person want to leave their current field?
2. Can they do this job, even without the usual background?
3. Will they stay, or will they leave when it gets hard?

Your letter answers each one. Give a short, positive reason for the move. Show transferable skills with real examples. Show commitment, such as a course you finished, a project, or volunteer work in the new field.

## How to Write a Career Change Cover Letter: Step by Step

### Step 1: Study the job post

List the main skills and tasks. Then mark the ones you have already done in another setting. A teacher moving into training has already planned lessons, presented to groups, and measured learning. A retail manager moving into operations has already handled schedules, inventory, and staff.

### Step 2: Translate your experience

Each field uses its own words. Rewrite your experience in the words of the new field.

| Old field | Your experience | In the new field's words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Teaching | Planned lessons for 30 students | Designed learning content for groups |
| Retail management | Managed weekly schedules for 15 staff | Workforce planning and scheduling |
| Hospitality | Handled guest complaints | Customer escalation and resolution |
| Nursing | Documented patient care in an EHR | Accurate records in a regulated system |
| Military | Led a team of 8 in logistics | Team leadership and supply chain operations |

Use these words in your letter and on your resume. The same words also help in your LinkedIn profile. The guide on [LinkedIn headline examples](/blog/linkedin-headline-examples) has a section for career changers.

### Step 3: Write a strong opening

Name the job and state the change in a confident way. Do not apologize for it.

Weak: "Although my background is in teaching, I hope you will consider me for this role."

Better: "Eight years of teaching taught me to explain hard ideas to busy people. I want to use that skill as a Customer Success Manager at [Company]."

The guide on [cover letter opening lines](/blog/cover-letter-opening-lines) has more examples.

### Step 4: Give two or three proof points

Pick the transferable skills that matter most for the job. For each, give one short example with a result. Mix examples from your old career and from your preparation for the new one.

### Step 5: Explain the "why" in one or two sentences

Keep it positive and focused on the future. "The data work was the part of my job I enjoyed most. So I completed a data analytics certificate and built three projects." Do not criticize your old field or employer.

### Step 6: Close with a clear request

Ask for a conversation, and thank the reader. The guide on [how to end a cover letter](/blog/how-to-end-a-cover-letter) has closing lines to choose from.

## Career Change Cover Letter Template

Replace each bracket. Cut any line that does not apply to you.

> Dear [Hiring Manager Name],
>
> I am applying for the [Job Title] role at [Company]. In [number] years as a [current or past role], I have [main transferable skill, with a short example], and I am ready to bring that experience to [new field].
>
> In my current role at [Current Company], I [specific achievement that maps to a need in the job post]. I also [second achievement that maps to another need]. These are the same skills your post lists for this role: [skill from post] and [skill from post].
>
> To prepare for this move, I [course, certificate, project, or volunteer work in the new field]. Through it, I [what you built or learned that applies to the job].
>
> I am drawn to [Company] because [specific reason]. I would welcome the chance to talk about how my background can help your team [goal from the job post]. Thank you for your time and consideration.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Your Full Name]
> [Phone Number] | [Email Address] | [LinkedIn URL]

## Example: Teacher to Instructional Designer

This is an example letter for a fictional person. Use it as a model for structure, not as text to copy.

> Dear [Hiring Manager Name],
>
> I am applying for the Instructional Designer role at [Company]. For seven years, I designed and taught a high school science course, and I want to bring that skill in building clear, engaging lessons to your corporate training team.
>
> As a teacher, I planned a full year of lessons, wrote assessments, and changed my materials when test results showed a gap. When my school moved to online learning, I rebuilt my course in our learning management system and trained four other teachers to do the same.
>
> To prepare for this change, I completed a course in instructional design and built two sample e-learning modules in Articulate Storyline. One module teaches new employees how to use a ticket system, and it is in my portfolio at [Portfolio URL].
>
> Your post mentions a plan to rebuild onboarding for new sales staff. I would welcome the chance to talk about how I can help with that project. Thank you for your time.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Your Full Name]

## Mistakes to Avoid

- Spending half the letter on why you left your old career.
- Apologizing for your background ("I know I do not have the usual experience").
- Using the words of your old field that the new reader will not understand.
- Listing courses without saying what you did with them.
- Sending a general letter. A career change letter must be specific, or the reader will not see the link.
- Claiming skills you cannot back up in an interview.

## Format and Length

Keep the letter to one page, about 250 to 400 words. A career change letter can run a little longer than average because you have more to explain. Use short paragraphs so the reader can scan it.

If you want a starting point, [CoverBetter](/) writes a first draft from your resume and the job post. Then add your reason for the change and your own proof points.

## Before You Send It

- Does the opening name the job and state your change with confidence?
- Did you translate your experience into the new field's words?
- Does each proof point connect to a need in the job post?
- Did you show commitment, such as a course, a project, or volunteer work?
- Is the "why" short and positive?

If the answer to each question is yes, the reader can see your fit without guessing. That is the main job of this letter.
