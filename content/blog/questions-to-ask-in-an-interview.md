The best questions to ask in an interview are about the work itself, how success is measured, the team, and the next steps. Good examples are "What does success look like in the first 90 days?" and "What is the biggest challenge for this team right now?". Prepare five to eight questions, then ask the two or three that fit the conversation best.

Your questions do two jobs. They help you decide whether you want the job. They also show the interviewer how you think. This guide gives you 40 questions grouped by topic, plus which ones fit each stage of the process.

## How to Choose Your Questions

Good questions are specific to the job and cannot be answered by reading the job post or the company website. Before the interview, read the post again, check the company's site, and look at the interviewer's LinkedIn profile. Then pick questions that fill the gaps.

Match your questions to the person you are talking to:

| Interviewer | Best topics |
| --- | --- |
| Recruiter | Process, timeline, team structure, pay range |
| Hiring manager | Goals, success measures, challenges, management style |
| Future teammates | Day-to-day work, tools, collaboration, culture |
| Senior leader | Company direction, priorities, how the team fits the plan |

## Questions to Ask in an Interview About the Role

1. What does a typical week look like in this role?
2. What does success look like in the first 90 days? In the first year?
3. What are the most important goals for this role in the next six months?
4. What is the biggest challenge someone in this role will face?
5. How has this role changed in the last year or two?
6. Is this a new role, or am I replacing someone? If replacing, what did that person do well?
7. Which skills do the strongest people on this team have in common?
8. What projects would I start on?

## Questions About the Team

9. How large is the team, and how is the work divided?
10. Who would I work with most closely, inside and outside the team?
11. How does the team share work and give updates?
12. What tools does the team use every day?
13. How does the team handle disagreements about priorities?
14. What do you like most about working with this team?
15. How do new people get up to speed here?

## Questions for the Hiring Manager

16. How would you describe your management style?
17. How often do you meet one-on-one with people on your team?
18. How do you give feedback?
19. What is something a past team member did that impressed you?
20. How do you measure performance for this role?
21. What would you want me to accomplish before my first review?

## Questions About Growth

22. What does growth look like for someone in this role?
23. Where have people in this role moved on to, inside or outside the company?
24. Does the company support training, courses, or conferences?
25. How are promotions decided?

## Questions About the Company and Culture

26. What do you enjoy most about working here?
27. How would you describe the culture in three words?
28. What are the company's top priorities this year?
29. How does this team's work connect to those priorities?
30. How has the company changed since you joined?
31. How does the company support work-life balance in practice?
32. How does the team work across time zones, if it is remote or hybrid?

## Questions About Next Steps

33. What are the next steps in the process?
34. When can I expect to hear back?
35. Is there anything else I can send you that would help?
36. Who else will I meet in the process?

## Questions That Show Confidence

These questions invite the interviewer to raise any doubts while you are still in the room. Ask them only if you are comfortable answering what you hear.

37. Is there anything about my background that makes you unsure about my fit?
38. What would make someone excellent in this role, not just good?
39. Based on our talk, what do you think I would find hardest in the first months?
40. What question should I have asked but did not?

## Questions to Ask at Each Stage

You do not need the same questions in every round.

- Recruiter or phone screen: ask about the team, the process, the timeline, and the pay range if it is not in the post. Some US states and cities require employers to share a pay range, so check your local rules.
- First interview with the hiring manager: ask about goals, success measures, and challenges (questions 2, 3, 4, 20).
- Panel or team interview: ask about the daily work, tools, and how the team works together (questions 1, 10, 11, 12).
- Final round: ask about priorities, growth, and anything that is still unclear (questions 22, 28, 37, 33).

## Questions to Avoid

Some questions send the wrong message, especially early in the process.

- Questions that the job post or the website already answers ("What does the company do?").
- "How soon can I get promoted?" in a first interview. Ask about growth instead.
- Questions about time off, perks, or remote work before you have shown interest in the work itself. Ask about them later, or ask the recruiter.
- "Did I get the job?" Ask about next steps instead.
- Questions about the interviewer's private life.
- Gossip about layoffs or problems you read about online. If it matters to you, ask in a neutral way: "How has the team changed over the last year?"

## How to Ask Your Questions Well

- Listen to the answer. A follow-up question shows more interest than a new topic.
- Take short notes. They help you write a specific thank-you email later.
- Watch the time. If the interviewer has five minutes left, ask your single most important question.
- Connect a question to your experience when it is natural: "At my last job, we used weekly demos to share progress. How does this team share work?"

If your interviewer already answered your questions, say so: "You covered most of what I wanted to ask. Can you say more about [topic they mentioned]?"

## Prepare Both Sides of the Conversation

Your questions are the last part of the interview, but they depend on the first part. Prepare your answers to the questions you will get, too. The guide on [common interview questions](/blog/common-interview-questions) covers the 20 you are most likely to hear, and the [STAR method interview](/blog/star-method-interview) guide helps you shape your stories.

After the interview, use the answers you heard. If the hiring manager told you the timeline, note it. If you do not hear back by then, the guide on the [follow up email after an interview](/blog/follow-up-email-after-interview) shows what to send.
