Fake job postings and job scams try to get your money or your personal information by offering a job that does not exist. The clearest warning signs are an offer without a real interview and a request to pay for anything. Others are a check you must deposit and send back, and contact only through text or chat apps. Before you share personal details, confirm the job on the company's official careers page and contact the company through its official website.

Job scams target people who want work fast, and that is most job seekers at some point. Scammers copy real company names, real logos, and real job titles. Some create fake recruiter profiles. The good news: most scams follow the same few patterns, and you can learn to see them.

## The Most Common Types of Job Scams

| Scam type | How it works | What they want |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fake check scam | They send you a check for "equipment" and ask you to send part of the money to a vendor. The check bounces later. | Your money |
| Pay to start | They ask for a fee for training, background checks, or a starter kit. | Your money |
| Identity theft | They ask for your Social Security number, bank details, or a copy of your ID before any real hiring process. | Your identity |
| Reshipping | You receive packages at home and send them to another address. The goods are often stolen. | Your address and your help with a crime |
| Fake recruiter | A "recruiter" offers a job and then asks for fees or personal data. | Money or data |
| Task or "click" jobs | You do simple online tasks, and then you must deposit money to "release" your earnings. | Your money |

The details change, but the goal is the same. A scam either takes money from you or takes information it can use later.

## Red Flags in Fake Job Postings

Look for these signs in the posting itself:

- The pay is very high for simple work or for no experience.
- The description is vague. It does not say what you will do each day.
- The posting has many spelling and grammar errors.
- The company name is missing, or you cannot find the company online.
- The contact email uses a free email service (for example, a Gmail or Yahoo address) instead of a company domain.
- The posting says "no interview needed" or "start today."
- The job asks you to apply through a chat app or by text message.

One sign alone does not prove a scam. Small companies sometimes write short posts. But two or three signs together are a strong reason to stop and check.

## Red Flags During the Hiring Process

Many job scams start with a normal-looking posting. The warning signs appear later:

- You get an offer after a short text chat, with no phone or video interview.
- The "interview" happens only by chat, and the interviewer avoids a call.
- They ask for your Social Security number, bank account, or ID photo before an offer in writing.
- They send you a check and ask you to buy equipment from their "approved vendor."
- They ask you to pay for anything: training, software, a background check, or a kit.
- They pressure you to act fast or say the offer ends today.
- The recruiter's email domain is slightly different from the company's real domain (for example, an extra letter or a different ending).
- They ask you to move the conversation off the job site to a private app.

The fake check scam is especially common. Your bank may make the money available before the check fully clears. When the check bounces, the bank can take the money back from your account, and you lose what you sent.

## How to Check If a Job Is Real

Use these steps before you share any personal details:

1. Go to the company's official website by typing the address yourself. Do not use links in the message.
2. Find the careers page. Check that the same job is listed there.
3. Look up the recruiter on LinkedIn. Check how long the profile has existed, their work history, and their connections at the company.
4. Compare the email domain with the domain of the company website.
5. Call or email the company through the contact details on its official website. Ask them to confirm the recruiter and the job.
6. Search the company name with words like "scam" or "fake recruiter."
7. Ask for a phone or video interview with a named person. Real employers expect this.

If any step fails, stop. A real employer will understand a careful question. A scammer will push you to skip the checks.

## What You Should Never Share Early

In a real hiring process, some information comes only after you accept a written offer, and only through the company's official HR system.

| Information | When a real employer usually asks for it |
| --- | --- |
| Social Security number | After an offer, for tax and payroll forms (some employers ask earlier for a background check, with your written consent) |
| Bank account details | After you start, for direct deposit |
| Copy of your ID or passport | After an offer, for employment eligibility forms |
| Date of birth | Usually after an offer, for benefits or background checks |
| Any payment from you | Almost never |

Rules about when an employer may ask for this information differ by state and by country. If you are unsure, ask the employer why they need it and how they will protect it.

## Protect Yourself While You Search

- Use a separate email address for job applications.
- Remove your home address and date of birth from your resume. A city and state are enough.
- Keep a record of every company you applied to, so you can recognize an unexpected "offer." A [job application tracker](/blog/job-application-tracker) helps.
- Do not deposit a check from an employer you have not met and confirmed.
- Do not pay money to get a job.
- Turn on two-factor login for your email and job site accounts.

Remote jobs are a common target, because scammers can skip in-person meetings. Our guide on [how to find remote jobs](/blog/how-to-find-remote-jobs) lists safer places to look.

## What to Do If You Think You Were Scammed

Act quickly. Speed can limit the damage.

1. Stop all contact with the scammer.
2. If you sent money, contact your bank or the payment app at once and ask if they can stop or reverse the payment.
3. If you shared your Social Security number or ID, go to [IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) to report it and get a recovery plan.
4. Change passwords for any accounts you shared or used with the scammer.
5. Report the scam to the Federal Trade Commission at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).
6. If the scam happened online, you can also report it to the FBI at [IC3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov/).
7. Report the fake posting or profile to the job board or to LinkedIn.
8. Save every message, email, and payment record.

You did not fail because a scammer targeted you. These schemes are designed to look real. A report helps protect other job seekers, so make one even if you lost nothing.

Outside the US, report job scams to your national consumer protection agency or the police.
